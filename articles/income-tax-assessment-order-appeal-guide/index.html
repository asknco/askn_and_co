Received an Income Tax Assessment Order? What It Means and How an Appeal Works

By ASKN & Co. LLP · 3 November 2026 · 8 min read

An assessment order with additions often arrives with a large demand, a notice of demand payable within 30 days, and a penalty notice on top. It is natural to be worried. The good news is that an assessment order is not the final word: a large share of additions are reduced or deleted in appeal, particularly where the Assessing Officer has not properly examined the evidence. What decides the outcome is how quickly and how carefully you act in the next few weeks.

This guide explains what the order means, the choices you have, and what an appeal actually involves.

First, understand what you have received

An assessment order usually comes with three documents. Read all three:

  1. The assessment order — the reasons for each addition or disallowance.
  2. The computation sheet and notice of demand under section 156 — the revised income, tax, interest and the amount payable, usually within 30 days.
  3. A penalty notice — very often, penalty proceedings for under-reporting or misreporting of income under section 270A are initiated at the same time.

Note the date on which the order was received. Almost every time limit runs from that date.

Your options

You are not limited to "pay or appeal". Depending on the case, the choices are:

  • Appeal to the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) — the usual remedy where you disagree with the additions on facts or law. It must be filed within 30 days of receiving the order.
  • Rectification under section 154 — for mistakes apparent from the record, such as TDS credit not given, a calculation error, or income counted twice. Rectification is quicker but cannot decide disputed questions. Filing for rectification does not extend the time to appeal, so where both apply, file both.
  • Immunity from penalty under section 270AA — if the additions are small or you are willing to accept them, you can apply, within one month from the end of the month in which you received the order, to pay the tax and interest, forgo the appeal and obtain immunity from penalty for under-reporting. This is not available where the order alleges misreporting. It is a strategic choice and should be weighed before the appeal deadline.
  • Revision by the Principal Commissioner under section 264 — an alternative to appeal in some cases, available only where no appeal has been filed.
  • Dispute Resolution Committee — an option for smaller taxpayers whose income and disputed additions are within the prescribed limits.

Choosing the right route at the start matters: some options close others.

What happens to the demand while the appeal is pending?

Filing an appeal does not automatically stop recovery. If the demand is not paid or stayed:

  • Interest under section 220(2) accrues at 1% per month.
  • Refunds due to you for other years can be adjusted against the demand.
  • In serious cases, the department can attach bank accounts or recover from your debtors.

To prevent this, apply to the Assessing Officer for a stay of demand until the appeal is decided. Under CBDT guidelines, a stay is generally granted on payment of 20% of the disputed demand. A lower payment can be sought where the additions are clearly unsustainable or there is genuine financial hardship, and a refusal can be taken to the Principal Commissioner. If your appeal succeeds, the amount paid is refunded with interest.

How an appeal before the CIT(A) works

Filing. The appeal is filed online in Form 35, with a statement of facts and grounds of appeal. The tax on the income you originally returned must already be paid. The fee is between ₹250 and ₹1,000, depending on the assessed income.

Grounds of appeal. Each addition is challenged as a separate ground, along with legal grounds — for example, that the notice or order was time-barred, or that you were not given a proper opportunity. Grounds left out at this stage can be difficult to raise later, so drafting them is not a formality.

Hearing. Most appeals are now heard by the faceless appeal unit through the e-filing portal. There is usually no face-to-face hearing, although a video hearing can be requested. In practice, this means the written submissions decide the case. They need to explain the facts clearly, connect every document to the issue it proves, deal with the reasoning of the Assessing Officer and rely on decisions of the jurisdictional High Court and the ITAT.

New evidence. Evidence that was not filed before the Assessing Officer can be admitted under rule 46A, but only if there was a good reason it could not be filed earlier. The application must explain that reason convincingly.

The CIT(A)'s powers. The CIT(A) can confirm, reduce or delete an addition — and can also enhance the assessment, after giving you notice. This is why an appeal must be prepared carefully, with an eye to issues the order did not raise.

Time. The law expects the CIT(A) to decide appeals within a year where possible, but in practice appeals can take longer. A stay of demand protects you in the meantime.

Where additions are commonly challenged successfully

In our litigation practice, additions are most often reduced or deleted where:

  • Cash deposits or credits were added as unexplained income under sections 68 to 69D — taxed at about 78% including surcharge and cess — and the source can be shown from withdrawals, recorded sales, loans or gifts with supporting evidence.
  • Additions were made purely from AIS data without verifying whether the income was already offered or belonged to someone else.
  • The order ignored the replies filed, or was passed without a show cause notice and draft order, or without enough time to respond.
  • Capital gains were recomputed on stamp duty value without referring the valuation to the Valuation Officer when you objected.
  • Expenses were disallowed for technical defaults that the law or the facts did not support.

Every case turns on its own record. A candid assessment of the strength of each ground — before filing — is part of good advice.

If the CIT(A) decides against you

You can appeal to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal within 60 days of receiving the CIT(A) order. The ITAT is the final authority on facts, and hearings before the Hyderabad Bench are in person. On a substantial question of law, a further appeal lies to the High Court. If the department appeals against an order in your favour, you can file cross-objections.

What to bring to your Chartered Accountant

To advise you properly within the 30-day window, we need:

  • The assessment order, computation sheet, notice of demand and penalty notice
  • Every notice received during the assessment and every reply filed
  • The return of income and computation for the year
  • The documents relevant to each addition — bank statements, agreements, invoices, confirmations
  • Any refund or adjustment intimations

Frequently asked questions

What is the time limit to appeal against an income tax assessment order?

An appeal to the CIT(A) must be filed within 30 days of receiving the order or notice of demand. A late appeal needs a condonation request explaining the delay.

Do I have to pay the tax demand if I file an appeal?

Filing an appeal does not stop recovery. Apply for a stay of demand; under CBDT guidelines a stay is generally granted on payment of 20% of the disputed demand, and a lower amount can be sought in suitable cases.

Can the CIT(A) increase my tax in an appeal?

Yes. The CIT(A) can enhance the assessment, but only after giving you notice and an opportunity to be heard.

Should I appeal or accept the order and seek penalty immunity?

If the additions are small and do not allege misreporting, applying for immunity under section 270AA — paying the tax and forgoing the appeal — can avoid penalty. It is a strategic choice to make before the appeal deadline.

How long does an income tax appeal take?

The CIT(A) is expected to decide appeals within a year where possible, but appeals often take longer. A stay of demand protects you in the meantime.

Section references are to the Income-tax Act, 1961, which continues to govern assessments and appeals for tax years up to 2025-26.
